## A Gentle Farewell at the Threshold of Eternity

If there is a passage in Western music capable of lifting earthly burdens and stripping death of its sting, it is the Adagio of Schubert’s String Quintet in C major, D. 956.

Composed in the autumn of 1828, barely two months before Schubert’s death at thirty-one, the piece departs from customary quintet scoring by adding a second cello rather than an extra viola. That second cello bestows upon the work a subterranean resonance and a motherly warmth unmatched in chamber literature.

## The Soul-Resonance of Twin Cellos

The partnership between Quatuor Ébène and cellist Gautier Capuçon yields a sublime marriage of precision and spontaneous combustion. In this Erato release, the arrival of the Adagio feels less like music entering a room and more like the atmospheric pressure dropping before twilight.

While the upper three strings float a hymn-like melody of disembodied serenity, the two cellos converse beneath—one offering pizzicato heartbeats, the other singing deep bass cantilenas. When the catastrophic, violent F minor middle section erupts, the Ébène musicians lean in with visceral theatrical fury before receding back into transcendental silence.

## Lingering Warmth in the Grooves

A longtime patron once confessed that whenever life collapsed into crisis, he surrendered himself to this very quintet. Schubert does not solve practical distress; he simply offers the profound reassurance of being utterly understood.

As Capuçon’s cello sighs into the final cadence alongside the quartet, you sense two wanderers embracing at dusk before parting into the mist.
