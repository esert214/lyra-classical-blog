## The Fatal Leap from Castel Sant'Angelo

Puccini's *Tosca* contains not a single superfluous note. From the three brutal opening fortissimo chords heralding Baron Scarpia's reign of terror, the opera's dramatic machinery hurtles like a runaway executioner's carriage toward its fatal reckoning.

At the dawn of the new millennium, EMI Classics captured what has become widely recognized as the modern benchmark recording. Taped at Abbey Road Studios under the volcanic direction of Antonio Pappano, the production united the reigning golden couple of the opera world—Angela Gheorghiu and Roberto Alagna—alongside the chillingly aristocratic Scarpia of Ruggero Raimondi, also serving as the official soundtrack to Benoît Jacquot's acclaimed feature film.

## Art, Love, and the Carving Knife

Gheorghiu's Tosca is the quintessence of the Roman diva: devout, wildly jealous, intensely sensual, and fiercely dangerous when cornered.

In Act II, within the shadowy confines of the Palazzo Farnese, as Scarpia turns Cavaradossi's torture into sexual blackmail, Pappano draws menacing, serpentine growls from the Covent Garden lower strings. Gheorghiu delivers a "Vissi d'arte" of heartbreaking fragility, whispering her bewilderment before blossoming into a soaring, incandescent plea.

And when she spots the carving knife on Scarpia's dining table, the orchestral crash that follows delivers an adrenaline shock right to the chest.

## Farewell Under the Roman Dawn

On the parapet of Castel Sant'Angelo in Act III, Alagna offers one of the defining interpretations of "E lucevan le stelle." As a lone clarinet laments through the misty dawn, Alagna's warm Mediterranean timbre aches with raw despair, mourning not abstract ideals, but the tactile memory of sweet kisses and soft flesh.

<!-- fact: Act III features the famous tenor aria 'E lucevan le stelle', introduced by a haunting solo clarinet melody -->

When the execution squad's blank cartridges turn out to be real bullets, Tosca takes her defiant leap from the battlements into eternity. Pappano unleashes the full orchestral force in a final roaring reprise of Cavaradossi's melody, sealing a timeless masterpiece that leaves the listener breathless.
