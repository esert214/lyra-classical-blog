## Can You Hear the Sound?

The Listeners. An opera about a group of people who begin to hear a mysterious low-frequency hum.

<!-- fact: The Listeners centers on a mysterious low-frequency hum heard by a group of people -->

Not a television. Not a neighbor's washing machine. A low, persistent sound "from below" that only some people can hear.

<!-- fiction: description of the opera's central image -->

Drawn to this sound, they gather, gradually drifting away from ordinary society to become some kind of underground community.

<!-- fact: The opera is based on the novel by Canadian writer Jordan Tannahill, exploring faith, community, and belonging -->

This is one of the most anticipated opera recordings of 2026—this world-premiere recording, led by soprano Nicole Heaston, is being released on Pentatone.

<!-- fact: Pentatone PTC 5187 530, released 2026, the world-premiere recording of The Listeners -->

## Who Is Listening?

Missy Mazzoli is one of the most important—and most anticipated—composers in contemporary American opera.

<!-- fact: Missy Mazzoli (b. 1980) is a leading figure in contemporary American opera -->

She is the first woman ever commissioned by the Metropolitan Opera—her Lincoln in the Bardo will premiere at the Met in autumn 2026.

<!-- fact: Mazzoli is the first woman commissioned by the Met; Lincoln in the Bardo premieres there in autumn 2026 -->

The Listeners is her latest collaboration with her longtime librettist, Royce Vavrek.

<!-- fact: Most of Mazzoli's operas are collaborations with librettist Royce Vavrek -->

The story begins with a middle-class housewife named Claire Devon. One day, she begins to hear the sound. From that day on, her life can never return to what it was.

<!-- fact: The protagonist Claire Devon is played by Nicole Heaston; the opera premiered at the Norwegian National Opera in 2022 and was staged in Chicago in 2025 -->

## The Low Frequency

Mazzoli uses double basses, percussion, and electronics to create that "audible but invisible" low-frequency hum at the heart of the opera.

<!-- fiction: description of compositional technique -->

It isn't terrifying. It's seductive—like a summons from somewhere deep that you cannot ignore.

<!-- fiction: listening interpretation -->

What makes this opera truly great is that it never tells you whether the sound is real or not. It only lets you follow Claire, and begin to wonder along with her: maybe that sound really does exist.

<!-- fiction: commentary on the work's openness -->

## Why Listen?

This is not a "beautiful" traditional opera. It is uneasy, eerie, yet strangely warm—much like real faith and a real sense of belonging, which are often contradictory.

<!-- opinion: subjective assessment -->

Heaston's Claire moves from confusion, to resistance, to final surrender, with a subtle and complete vocal arc. You follow her, down into that world "below."

<!-- fiction: commentary on the singing -->

And—this is an opera about "hearing a sound that others cannot." In an age of information overload and growing loneliness, this theme hits closer to home than ever.

<!-- fiction: commentary on its contemporary relevance -->
