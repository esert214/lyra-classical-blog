## A Paris Winter at Four Degrees

La Bohème takes place in winter. Bitter, unheated, where only love can keep you warm. In that Left Bank garret, four artists survive on "a glass of wine, a pen, a melody."

<!-- fact: The opera is set in 1830s Paris' Latin Quarter; La Bohème premiered in Turin in 1896 -->

Puccini wrote that coldness into the opera's most famous warmth. In Act I, Che gelida manina — "Your tiny hand is frozen" — Rodolfo takes Mimì's hand: few arias in opera history warm both the characters and the entire audience like this one.

<!-- fact: Che gelida manina from Act I is one of the most famous melodies of the whole opera -->

## A Recording That Never Rushes

Pappano's 2006 EMI recording with the Philharmonia Orchestra casts Alagna and Vaduva: the reigning French-Italian tenor of the moment, and a Romanian soprano often called a born Mimì.

<!-- fact: EMI album released June 19, 2006; Rodolfo is sung by Roberto Alagna, Mimì by Leontina Vaduva, with Philip Sheffield as Marcello and Ruth Ann Swenson as Musetta, plus London Voices and the Wandsworth School Boys' Choir -->

Pappano's conducting never shows off. He doesn't treat the prelude as fireworks; he lets the orchestra breathe like a room — brass coldest, woodwinds softest, and strings, in Act IV when everyone walks toward a different door, warm as if unwilling to let go.

<!-- fiction: impressionistic description of Pappano's conducting style -->

<!-- fact: In Act IV Mimì dies in Rodolfo's arms — one of opera's most universally acknowledged heart-breaking endings -->

## Why Collect This One Now?

Recordings of La Bohème could stack a Eiffel Tower. Pappano's stands out for two reasons: the Philharmonia's sparkly British sonority that nonetheless defers to Italian song; and the chemistry between Alagna and Vaduva — so real it feels performed, so performed it feels real.

<!-- fiction: lyrical summary of the bel canto tradition -->

<!-- fact: In 2006 Pappano was already an acknowledged authority on Italian opera; critics widely rate this Bohème among the best-recorded modern versions -->

## Mimì, and Every Girl Behind Her

An opera survives 130 years on more than melody. Mimì endures because she coughs, embroiders, and tells Rodolfo "I'll wait for you in spring" — she is every person who still believes in spring amid winter.

<!-- fiction: the literary elevation of Mimì's image -->

<!-- fact: The characters derive from Henri Murger's novel Scènes de la vie de bohème -->

## Further Listening

If you like your Puccini modern, Sondra Radvanovsky's aria album Puccini Heroines sweeps up the great arias of Mimì, Tosca, and Butterfly. For the most "original" classic, the 1959 Tebaldi/Bergonzi/Serafin Decca recording remains a bible for many listeners — hear the modern version first, then the old one, and let the two "colds" fight in your ears. That is the joy of classical music.
