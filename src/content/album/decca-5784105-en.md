## Mozart Was Never a "Serious Person"

When I learned piano as a kid, my teacher placed Mozart on a shrine.

<!-- fiction: opening with childhood piano-lesson experience for emotional connection -->

The tone had to be clean, the ornaments elegant, and you were absolutely forbidden from adding anything of your own. The opening "do-mi-sol-mi" of K. 545 sounded identical in every practice room in Taiwan — as uniform as schoolchildren lined up in formation.

<!-- fiction: describing the stereotyped image of Mozart in Chinese piano pedagogy -->

But here's the thing: Mozart himself was a prankster who loved to improvise and slipped jokes into his music. His ornaments were originally blank spaces left for performers to fill in on the spot. His variation sets were literally him "playing around" with other people's tunes.

<!-- fact: Mozart was a great improviser and many works grew out of improvisations later written down; his variations K. 265 are based on the French folksong 'Ah! vous dirai-je, maman' (the Twinkle Twinkle melody) -->

What Jon Batiste does on Black Mozart can be summed up in one sentence: he takes Mozart down from the shrine and brings him to a New Orleans bar for a drink.

<!-- fiction: the central metaphor of the piece — Mozart meeting the jazz bar -->

## A Conversation Recorded in a Single Day

This is the second installment of the Batiste Piano Series. The first was 2024's Beethoven Blues; this time it's Mozart's turn.

<!-- fact: this album is Batiste Piano Series Vol. 2 (Decca Records US, released August 14, 2026), following Vol. 1 Beethoven Blues (2024) -->

Ten tracks, thirty-five minutes, all solo piano. Batiste says he had played Beethoven half his life and knew it with his eyes closed; but Mozart was territory he was entering for the first time — on recording day he spread out the scores, played through each theme once, and improvised his response on the spot, finishing the whole album in a single day.

<!-- fact: the album has 10 tracks of about 35 minutes, all solo piano; Batiste has said the recording-day playthroughs were his first deep engagement with Mozart, responding to each theme by improvisation -->

The opener "Facile-Batiste" takes a knife to the first movement of K. 545: for a few seconds you still recognize that "do-mi-sol-mi," and the next second it slides into a blues bend — the Viennese salon instantly becomes the American South of the 1920s.

<!-- fact: opening track 'Facile-Batiste' reworks the first movement of Mozart's Piano Sonata in C major K. 545 with jazz and blues vocabulary -->

## The Two Lives of Twinkle Twinkle

My favorites are the twins, "Shine" and "Twinkle."

<!-- fiction: personal listening preference -->

They come from the same song — Mozart's twelve variations K. 265 on a French folksong, the tune the whole world knows as Twinkle Twinkle Little Star. Batiste gives one melody two lives: one that shines, one that twinkles, one in daylight, one deep at night.

<!-- fact: 'Shine' and 'Twinkle' are both drawn from Mozart's variations K. 265 on 'Ah! vous dirai-je, maman' -->

And "Country Zart" is bolder still: it plugs the Marriage of Figaro Overture into the driving pulse of Black country music. Before the opera-house chandelier stops swaying, you're already out on the highway.

<!-- fact: 'Country Zart' fuses the Marriage of Figaro Overture with Black country music elements -->

## Why "Black Mozart"

The title will surely raise some eyebrows. But Batiste is disarmingly frank about it: as a teenager in New Orleans he spent his days in classical piano competitions and his nights in jazz clubs, until he heard jazz clarinet master Alvin Batiste — a distant cousin — blow a beloved Mozart melody as a twelve-bar blues in his cousin's band. That's when he understood: Mozart never belonged to one skin color or one social class.

<!-- fact: as a teenager in New Orleans Batiste lived between classical competitions by day and jazz clubs by night; hearing his distant cousin, jazz clarinetist Alvin Batiste, reinvent a Mozart melody as a twelve-bar blues profoundly influenced him -->

He argues that the great classical canon — Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin, Bach — has reached the point where it needs "updating." Not out of disrespect, but precisely out of reverence: every musical tradition since deserves its conversation with the canon. The blues has something to say. Jazz has something to say. Gospel has something to say.

<!-- fact: Batiste holds that the classical canon should be brought into dialogue with later traditions including the blues and jazz; the album's vocabulary spans jazz, rags, stride, blues, and stomps -->

The closing "Gospel Andante" is the period at the end of that belief: andante tempo, gospel harmony, like a congregation lingering at the church door after the service, singing one more chorus because nobody wants to leave.

<!-- fiction: literary depiction of the finale's atmosphere -->

## By the Way: He Released Three Albums That Day

On August 14, 2026, Batiste actually released three Piano Series albums at once: this Black Mozart (Vol. 2, on Decca), plus two tributes to Thelonious Monk — Monk Meditations (Vol. 3) and Monk Movements (Vol. 4), both on Verve.

<!-- fact: three Piano Series albums were released on August 14, 2026: Black Mozart (Decca) plus Monk Meditations and Monk Movements (Verve) -->

One man, one piano: an afternoon chatting with Mozart, then turning around to chat with Monk. That kind of stamina — well, eight Grammys don't win themselves.
