## Moscow, 1936: A Suitcase by the Door

In 1936, Dmitri Shostakovich never undressed before going to sleep.

<!-- fact: Following the Pravda editorial "Muddle Instead of Music" denouncing Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk, Shostakovich lived in terror of arrest and frequently waited by his apartment elevator fully dressed with a packed suitcase -->

He kept a small packed bag resting by the door. Night after night, he waited for the footsteps of the NKVD, praying only that if he were dragged into the night, it would happen without waking his sleeping wife and infant daughter. Under this asphyxiating shadow, he wrote his most monstrous and uncompromising masterpiece, the Fourth Symphony—only to lock the score in a drawer for a quarter of a century.

<!-- fact: Shostakovich withdrew Symphony No. 4 during rehearsals in 1936; it received its premiere only in 1961 under Kirill Kondrashin -->

Finnish podium phenomenon Klaus Mäkelä and the Oslo Philharmonic present Symphonies 4, 5, and 6 as a singular monumental triptych on Decca Classics. At twenty-eight, Mäkelä approaches these scores free from conservatory mannerisms, staring down the abyss with icy Scandinavian clarity.

<!-- fact: Decca released Mäkelä and the Oslo Philharmonic's 2-CD set of Shostakovich Symphonies 4, 5, and 6 in August 2024 (catalog 485 4637) -->

## The Hollow Rejoicing of the Fifth

Symphony No. 5 was famously labeled "a Soviet artist's creative response to just criticism."

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 5 triumphed at its 1937 Leningrad premiere, accepted by the regime as a return to order, though Shostakovich later noted its rejoicing was "forced" -->

Its thunderous brass chords and relentless timpani rolls in the finale were once routinely played as unironic socialist triumphalism. Under Mäkelä, however, the brass gleaming from the Oslo orchestra reflects the sickly sheen of cold steel. The tempo is deliberately unyielding, hammer blow upon hammer blow, like nails driven into bone.

<!-- fiction: characterizing Mäkelä's chillingly bleak interpretation of the finale -->

This is not celebration; this is a man with a pistol pressed to his temple, bleeding and ordered to dance on the public square shouting that he has never been happier. Mäkelä's restraint makes the terror all the more inescapable.

<!-- fiction: metaphor of totalitarian coercion within the score -->

## The Meat Grinder of the Fourth and the Burlesque Sixth

If the Fifth represents a public survival strategy, the Fourth is the untamed leviathan.

<!-- fiction: contrasting the wild modernism of the Fourth with the structured facade of the Fifth -->

A colossal orchestra of over one hundred musicians hurls itself into the central fugue of the first movement like an industrial meat grinder. The Oslo strings articulate the frantic counterpoint with surgical menace. And when the finale subsides, the celesta ticks over a pedal point in C minor for minutes on end, leaving the hall looking out upon a post-nuclear wasteland.

<!-- fact: The Fourth concludes with an eerie, extended fadeout featuring solitary celesta strokes over a pedal C minor chord -->

The Sixth then upturns all classical form: a sprawling, twenty-minute tragic Largo followed immediately by two manic, circus-like gallops.

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 6 dispenses with an opening fast movement, juxtaposing a desolate Largo with an anarchic Scherzo and Presto -->

Walking out into the evening after hearing this set, you can't help glancing back over your shoulder. Music is not always meant to comfort us; sometimes it exists to bear witness to how humans kept breathing through the darkest hour.
