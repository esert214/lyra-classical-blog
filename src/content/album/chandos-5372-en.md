## Forty-Five Years in One Man

How can two entirely different kinds of music live on the same album?

<!-- fiction: opening question, literary device -->

Arvo Pärt's First Symphony was written in 1963, in the most avant-garde serialist language of its day. His Fourth was written in 2008, in his own tintinnabuli—that bell-like, radically simple, sacred sound.

<!-- fact: Pärt's four symphonies span 1963–2008; No. 1 is a serialist-period work, No. 4 is a tintinnabuli-period work -->

Forty-five years. Two languages. One soul.

<!-- fiction: literary summation -->

This complete cycle, recorded by the Iceland Symphony Orchestra under Eva Ollikainen, sets both voices side by side, so you can hear Pärt's entire symphonic journey in one sitting.

<!-- fact: Chandos CHSA 5372, released 2026, containing all four symphonies -->

## No. 1 — The Years of Experiment

In the 1960s, Pärt was still searching for his voice. He wrote in twelve-tone technique, producing music of enormous technical complexity.

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 1 (1963) is a representative serialist-period work -->

The First Symphony is like a self-portrait of that period—tense, intellectual, marked by a young man's devotion to "the new." Ollikainen keeps the Iceland Symphony's playing austere and precise, without overstatement.

<!-- fiction: description of the interpretation -->

But even in these most avant-garde notes, you can already faintly hear the later Pärt—between the fractured melodic lines hides a longing for some pure, essential sound.

<!-- fiction: reading of the work's internal trajectory -->

## Nos. 2 & 3 — The Turning

Symphonies Two and Three are Pärt's transitional works. He gradually left serialism behind, seeking a "simpler" music—one that could carry faith and silence.

<!-- fact: No. 2 (1966) and No. 3 (1971) belong to Pärt's transitional period from serialism toward minimalism -->

The ending of the Third Symphony is especially moving. After all the complexity, the music suddenly settles into a quiet, almost prayerful texture.

<!-- fiction: listening description of the structure -->

At that moment you understand: Pärt didn't abandon modernity. He passed through it, and came out the other side.

<!-- fiction: commentary on Pärt's style -->

## No. 4 — Bells of Los Angeles

The Fourth Symphony, "Los Angeles," was written in 2008. It is the latest and best-known of the four.

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 4 (2008) carries the subtitle 'Los Angeles' -->

Its musical language is fully mature. The bell-like tintinnabuli, the vast sense of space, the religious stillness—this is Pärt at his purest.

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 4 is a typical example of Pärt's tintinnabuli style -->

In Ollikainen's reading, the orchestra's strings glow warm and translucent. Those high-register bell rings float like aurora over an Icelandic sky—at once distant and near.

<!-- fiction: aurora metaphor for the timbre -->

## Why Iceland?

There is a strange rightness in the Iceland Symphony Orchestra playing Pärt.

<!-- opinion: subjective impression -->

Pärt comes from Estonia—a small, cold Baltic nation. Iceland, too, is an island nation in the North Atlantic. Both share that northern quality of sound: at once lonely and pure.

<!-- fiction: cultural association -->

And since 2020, the Iceland Symphony has been led by Eva Ollikainen, its first woman chief conductor. She brings her own warmth and breathing to these northern bells.

<!-- fact: Eva Ollikainen has been chief conductor of the Iceland Symphony since 2020, the first woman in the role -->

## Further Listening

Pärt's music holds a spiritual dialogue with another Eastern European contemporary, György Kurtág—both touch the depths of the human soul in radically minimal and profound language. Pierre-Laurent Aimard's Játékok is a superb companion.
