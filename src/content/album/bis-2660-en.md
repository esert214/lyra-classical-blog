## A Knock on Schumann’s Door

On the morning of September 30, 1853, a dusty twenty-year-old youth from Hamburg stood before an apartment in Düsseldorf, carrying a worn knapsack.

His name was Johannes Brahms. When Robert Schumann opened the door and ushered the young man to the upright piano, Schumann expected polite courtesy. But when Brahms’s hands struck the monumental opening chords of his *Piano Sonata No. 1 in C major*, Op. 1—reminiscent of Beethoven's Fifth—Schumann leapt to his feet and called for his wife Clara: "Come, Clara, listen! Here is someone who will change music forever!"

<!-- fact: In autumn 1853, the 20-year-old Brahms visited the Schumanns; Robert subsequently published his famous essay 'Neue Bahnen' (New Paths) heralding Brahms as a genius destined to lead music into the future -->

Over a century and a half later, French virtuoso Alexandre Kantorow—widely celebrated as a modern reincarnation of Liszt—recorded this program for BIS Records, claiming the **2025 Gramophone Piano Award**.

<!-- fact: Released on BIS Records (catalogue BIS-2660 SACD), the album won the 2025 Gramophone Piano Award, pairing Brahms's First Sonata with Schubert's Wanderer Fantasy and Liszt song transcriptions -->

## An Orchestral Volcano Veiled in Ivory

Listening to Kantorow navigate Brahms Op. 1, what takes the breath away is the bottomless depth of his touch.

He never bangs; rather, he channels his entire upper body weight through firm knuckles directly into the keybed. The massive octaves that mimic a full orchestral *tutti* resound with dark bronze resonance. Yet in the second movement variations on the old German folk song *Verstohlen geht der Mond auf*, his tone dissolves instantly into pale moonlight reflected in a castle moat—pure, chaste, and imbued with quiet yearning.

<!-- fiction: Contrasting Kantorow's volcanic orchestral gravitas with the delicate intimacy of the slow movement -->

In Schubert’s *Wanderer Fantasy* D 760, Kantorow unleashes breathtaking keyboard mastery. This was the terrifying score over which Schubert himself famously threw up his hands in despair, muttering: "Let the devil play it!" Across its four interconnected movements, Kantorow turns the work into a metaphysical pilgrimage.

<!-- fact: Composed in 1822, Schubert's Wanderer Fantasy is notoriously difficult, using a single cyclical motif derived from his lied 'Der Wanderer' across four continuous movements -->

In the Adagio ("I come from the mountains, the valleys are dim, the sea sighs..."), his piano descends into somber baritone depths, capturing the 19th-century wanderer's eternal quest for home.

<!-- fiction: Evoking the existential solitude of Schubert's wanderer -->

## The Youthful Giant’s Long Road

The album concludes with five of Liszt's luminous Schubert song transcriptions, including *Am Meer* and *Die Stadt*. Here, Kantorow makes the piano sing with human vocal inflection, breathing through every phrase like a lieder singer.

<!-- fact: Kantorow won the Tchaikovsky Competition Gold Medal and Grand Prix in 2019 and received the Gilmore Artist Award in 2024; he performed in Taiwan in December 2024 and May 2026 -->

This is not an album to play as casual background music. It asks for an uninterrupted hour in a quiet room, walking in the footsteps of a twenty-seven-year-old master who channels both the fiery bravado of young Brahms and the heartbreaking wanderlust of Schubert.

<!-- fiction: Wally Records closing reflection on finding a home in music -->

When the final resonant chord fades into the listening room, one understands the tears in Schumann’s eyes that September morning: the profound shudder of an artistic spirit arriving safely home.
