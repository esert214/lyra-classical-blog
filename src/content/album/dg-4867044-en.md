## The Grandest Beast of the 20th Century

Is there a twentieth-century symphony that can make you uncomfortable and breathtakingly beautiful at the same time?

<!-- fiction: opening question -->

The answer is Messiaen's Turangalîla-Symphonie.

<!-- fiction: answer after a rhetorical question -->

Written in 1946–48, the work demands a huge orchestra, plus a piano, an ondes Martenot, and a performance lasting about eighty minutes.

<!-- fact: Turangalîla-Symphonie was written 1946–48, requiring a large orchestra, piano, and ondes Martenot, lasting about 80 minutes -->

Its name comes from Sanskrit: turanga (speed) and lîla (game)—meaning "song of love" or "song of life."

<!-- fact: The title Turangalîla derives from the Sanskrit turanga and lîla, meaning 'song of love' -->

And this recording brings together Yuja Wang, the Boston Symphony Orchestra, and Andris Nelsons—the very orchestra that gave the world premiere in 1949.

<!-- fact: The Boston Symphony gave the world premiere of Turangalîla in 1949; this recording features Yuja Wang on piano and Nelsons conducting -->

## Yuja Wang's Piano

In Turangalîla, the piano is not an afterthought. It is a core of virtuosity and emotion, carrying an eighty-minute dialogue with the orchestra.

<!-- fact: The piano is a major solo role in Turangalîla, in dialogue with the orchestra -->

Yuja Wang—the pianist renowned for virtuosity combined with artistry—shows astonishing structural sense here. Those glittering, waterfall-like cascades are handled with both virtuosic thrill and musical logic.

<!-- fiction: commentary on Wang's playing -->

Hear in the first movement the piano and orchestra chasing each other, like two great energies racing to the summit.

<!-- fiction: description of the music -->

## The Ondes Martenot

Turangalîla's most beguiling sonority comes from the ondes Martenot—an early electronic instrument of the last century.

<!-- fact: The ondes Martenot is an early electronic instrument, a major solo role in Turangalîla -->

Its sound hovers between voice, strings, and siren—at once gentle and uncanny, with an otherworldly quality. Cécile Lartigau's playing makes those glissandi drift through the orchestra like phantoms.

<!-- fiction: description of the ondes Martenot's timbre -->

## Love and Light

Messiaen was a devout Catholic; his music always sings of the sacred. But Turangalîla's subject is "love" itself—human, passionate, bodily love.

<!-- fact: Messiaen was a devout Catholic; Turangalîla celebrates human and passionate love -->

The fifth movement's "Joie du sang des étoiles" (Joy of the Blood of the Stars) is the ecstatic peak of the whole work—an explosion of rhythm and color, as if the entire universe were celebrating.

<!-- fact: The fifth movement, 'Joie du sang des étoiles', is the ecstatic peak of the work -->

Nelsons releases astonishing energy from the Boston Symphony while keeping the structure clear. This is not a chaotic revel—it is ordered, solemn joy.

<!-- fiction: commentary on Nelsons's conducting -->
