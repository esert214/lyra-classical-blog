## A Trip That Took Thirteen Years

In 1829, twenty-year-old Mendelssohn set foot in Scotland.

<!-- fact: Mendelssohn visited Scotland in 1829 -->

He was deeply moved by the landscape, especially the ruins of Holyrood Palace in Edinburgh—where, it is said, the opening theme of this symphony first came to him.

<!-- fact: The opening theme is said to have come to him among the ruins of Holyrood Palace in Edinburgh -->

But he spent a full thirteen years writing the symphony, not completing it until 1842.

<!-- fact: The Third Symphony was conceived in 1829 and completed in 1842, a span of about 13 years -->

Scotland's mists, the weight of history, and a young man's longing for distant places—all are sealed by Mendelssohn into this symphony.

<!-- fiction: interpretation of the work's emotion -->

## The Opening in the Mist

The symphony's opening is one of the most famous moments in the whole piece.

<!-- fact: The Third Symphony opens with a famous slow introduction -->

The low strings murmur, joined by woodwinds and harp, creating a moist, hazy atmosphere characteristic of the Scottish Highlands. Lahav Shani keeps this opening in exactly the right stillness—not mystery, but the patience of waiting in fog.

<!-- fiction: description of the opening music and performance -->

Then the theme emerges. It carries a Scottish folk color, evolving and developing throughout the movement.

## Rotterdam's Warmth

This album earned a 2025 pick from The Classic Review, which praised it as an "emotional satisfaction" for listeners tired of period-instrument performances.

<!-- fact: This album was a 2025 pick by The Classic Review -->

In recent years many conductors have favored period instruments or historical approaches for Mendelssohn. Shani does not. He lets the Rotterdam Philharmonic, with modern instruments and rich tone, release Mendelssohn's romance and passion fully.

<!-- opinion: commentary on the interpretative approach -->

This is not archaeology. It is making the music come alive.

## Movement II — Bagpipes

The second movement is a signature of the symphony. Here Mendelssohn imitates Scottish bagpipes—not literally, but with clarinets and bassoons reproducing that melody over a sustained drone.

<!-- fact: The second movement imitates Scottish bagpipes, using woodwinds like the clarinet over a sustained drone -->

Shani makes this movement light and rustic with joy, like a festival in the Highlands.

<!-- fiction: interpretation of the second movement -->

## Finale — Homecoming

The fourth movement is the symphony's triumph. It moves from a chaotic, warlike opening to a final solemn and radiant conclusion.

<!-- fact: The fourth movement moves from a violent opening to a solemn, radiant ending -->

Some scholars see this finale as symbolizing the glory of a Scottish national hero. Whatever the symbolism, Shani brings this ending to a true "epic" height—grand, affirmative, yet carrying a deep stillness.

<!-- fiction: interpretation of the finale -->

## Why Listen

This is an underrated symphony. Among Mendelssohn's works, it is often overshadowed by the Italian.

<!-- opinion: subjective commentary -->

But the Scottish has greater depth—it fuses the romance of a travelogue, the weight of history, and the brilliance of the finale. Shani and the Rotterdam Philharmonic attend to every side of it just right.

<!-- opinion: subjective recommendation -->

If you want a symphony recording that is an "emotional satisfaction," this is it.

<!-- opinion: subjective recommendation -->
