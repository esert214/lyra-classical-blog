## Five Mysterious Dots upon the Title Page

In the early autumn of 1910, among the rolling green hills of Worcestershire, fifty-three-year-old Edward Elgar completed the most expansive, deeply personal score of his life: the *Violin Concerto in B minor*, Op. 61.

Across the dedication page, he inscribed a cryptic sentence in Spanish from Cervantes: "Aquí está encerrada el alma de ....." (Herein is enshrined the soul of .....).

Who was concealed behind those five enigmatic dots? A departed Edwardian golden age? Or his soul-muse Alice Stuart-Wortley, whom he affectionately nicknamed "Windflower"? Elgar took the secret to his grave, confiding only to a close friend: "It's full of something we all feel, isn't it? I have written my soul into it."

<!-- fact: Elgar completed his Violin Concerto in 1910, dedicated to Fritz Kreisler; the five dots are widely believed by scholars to refer to Alice Stuart-Wortley -->

Over a century later, Norwegian violinist Vilde Frang joined forces with conductor Robin Ticciati and the Deutsches Symphonie-Orchester Berlin to create a recording on Warner Classics that captured the **2025 Gramophone Concerto Award**.

<!-- fact: Released by Warner Classics (catalogue 2173240942), the recording won the 2025 Gramophone Concerto Award, featuring the concerto alongside Elgar's charming miniature Carissima -->

## A Silver Filament in the Autumn Wind

Listeners reared on the imperial nobility of Heifetz or the youthful fire of Menuhin will be struck immediately by Frang’s unexpected vulnerability.

She does not treat this fifty-minute monolith as a suit of brass armor. When her solo entrance emerges after the three-minute orchestral exposition, it is not a declaration of mastery, but a trembling whisper in an autumn gust.

<!-- fiction: Evoking Frang's fragile, introspective tone and poetic intimacy -->

Her tone shimmers like gossamer caught in morning mist; her *pianissimi* are so tender one holds one's breath. Ticciati’s direction is equally revelatory: having spent a decade at Glyndebourne, he shapes the DSO Berlin with the supple, breathing elasticity of a great vocal accompanist.

<!-- fact: Ticciati brings his deep operatic experience to bear on the orchestral pacing, maintaining chamber transparency throughout -->

The emotional peak arrives in the finale's unprecedented *Cadenza accompagnata*. Frang hovers gently above the fingerboard, while the orchestral strings "thrum" with the soft pads of their fingers (*pizzicato tremolando*), mimicking the sound of distant waves breaking on an empty shore. Amidst this nocturnal hush, Frang looks back across all the concerto's earlier themes—like a traveler boarding a night ship, casting one last lingering gaze upon a receding homeland.

<!-- fact: Elgar invented the 'cadenza accompagnata' for this concerto, where strings softly accompany the solo cadenza with a unique tremolando effect -->

## An Amber Enshrinement

The disc concludes with a gentle three-minute encore, *Carissima*, performed with pianist Thomas Hoppe, folding away the emotional turbulence of the concerto like clean white linen.

<!-- fact: Carissima was composed by Elgar in 1913, becoming his very first commercial acoustic recording for HMV in 1914 -->

At the end of an exhausting day, this album is a sanctuary. The soul Elgar locked away over a hundred years ago steps into the room, reawakened by the silver fingers of a Norwegian artist.

<!-- fiction: Wally Records closing reflection on memory, love, and solace -->

In the glow of a rainy evening, Frang’s long, unbroken lines remind us of a timeless truth: those we have loved deeply never truly depart; they remain enshrined forever in the enduring cadence of great art.
