## A Concert Inside the Siege

September 1941. German armies encircle Leningrad.

<!-- fact: The siege of Leningrad began in September 1941 and lasted nearly 900 days -->

Over the following nine hundred days, nearly a million people starved to death in the city. And in that darkest winter, the score of Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony was photographed onto microfilm and smuggled out past enemy lines—then performed by the London Philharmonic in London, its first performance outside Russia.

<!-- fact: The score was smuggled out on microfilm; the LPO gave the UK premiere in summer 1942—the work's first performance outside Russia -->

Yes, you read that right: **the London Philharmonic**.

So when Kurt Masur stood at the Royal Festival Hall conducting this symphony in 2003, it was not just another performance—the orchestra was playing music written into its own history.

<!-- fact: This recording is live from December 13, 2003 at the Royal Festival Hall; Masur was then LPO Principal Conductor -->

## The Invasion Theme

The famous "invasion march" of the first movement repeats the same melody eleven times.

<!-- fact: The invasion theme repeats the same melody many times over a crescendo—one of classical music's most famous builds -->

It starts as a whisper from a distant snare drum. Each repetition grows louder, closer, more impossible to ignore—until the whole orchestra thunders like war itself crushing a city.

<!-- fiction: description of the invasion theme's sound -->

Some said it depicted the Nazis; Shostakovich himself said it was really about all forces that trample human dignity.

<!-- fact: Shostakovich indicated the theme condemned all tyranny, not only the Nazis -->

## Masur: The Man Who Helped Topple a Wall

Kurt Masur's name is tied to things beyond music.

<!-- fact: Masur (1927–2015) was one of the most important German conductors of the late twentieth century -->

In autumn 1989, on the eve of the Berlin Wall's fall, he walked out of Leipzig's Gewandhaus and stood before the crowds calling for peace—he was one of the key figures who allowed East Germany's transition without bloodshed.

<!-- fact: During the 1989 Leipzig demonstrations Masur spoke with citizens to enable a peaceful transition, earning credit for the peaceful reunification -->

He led the Gewandhaus for 26 years, then the New York Philharmonic for 11. And Taiwanese audiences remember him directly: in 1998 he led the NY Philharmonic's first Taiwan visit—Taipei and Kaohsiung each hosted a concert; he returned to Taipei in 2002; and in 2010 he brought the Orchestre National de France to Taipei and Tainan.

<!-- fact: Masur visited Taiwan in 1998 (NY Phil Taipei + Kaohsiung), 2002 (NY Phil Taipei), and 2010 (ONF Taipei + Tainan) -->

He died in the United States in December 2015, aged 88. This recording from 2003 remains a precious testament to him and the LPO.

<!-- fact: Masur died on December 19, 2015 in Connecticut, USA -->

## Why Listen

Critics describe Masur's Leningrad as "more Mahler than Shostakovich"—that cold chordal wind at the start of the slow movement blows through ruins.

<!-- opinion: citing Qobuz review viewpoint -->

If you hear this symphony only once, this is a choice with both historical weight and live warmth.

<!-- opinion: subjective recommendation -->
