## A Candle in Paris That Blows Out on Cue

In a drafty garret above the Latin Quarter of 1830s Paris, the fire in the stove has long turned to ash.

<!-- fact: Act I of La Bohème is set in a frigid Latin Quarter garret where Rodolfo and his companions burn manuscripts to keep warm -->

Rodolfo is writing alone when a knock sounds at the door. Outside stands a seamstress, her candle unlit, her door key lost in the dark, her fingers cold as marble. Puccini's most mercilessly romantic stroke is right here: a draft blows out the flame, leaving two strangers groping on the floorboards until their hands touch in the pitch dark.

<!-- fiction: romantic tension of the candle extinguishing in the garret -->

In this 2020 production from the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, Bulgarian soprano Sonya Yoncheva embodies Mimì not as a frail, sickly cliché, but as a full-blooded woman intoxicated by life.

<!-- fact: This film captures Richard Jones's acclaimed production at the Royal Opera House, recorded in January 2020 and released on Opus Arte OA1332D -->

When she sings "Sì, mi chiamano Mimì," her voice is rich, warm, and defiant. You do not hear a doomed consumptive ready to surrender; you hear a girl standing on the edge of spring, stitching silk roses to keep death at bay.

<!-- fiction: interpreting Yoncheva's vocal warmth as an assertion of life -->

## Charles Castronovo's Rodolfo: Broke, Reckless, and Alive

Opposite her, American tenor Charles Castronovo delivers a Rodolfo of authentic Mediterranean passion.

<!-- fact: Charles Castronovo stars as Rodolfo; his Italian-American heritage brings an idiomatic bel canto sheen to the role -->

When he takes Mimì's hand for "Che gelida manina," his radiant high C rings out through Covent Garden without hesitation. In that moment, poverty ceases to be a shame and becomes the proudest badge of youth—they do not have two sous to rub together, yet they own the stars above Paris.

<!-- fiction: romanticizing the bohemian defiance of poverty through high vocal drama -->

Director Richard Jones strips away all sentimental clutter. Stark winter trees, blanketing snow, and the chilly mist outside the Café Momus serve as relentless reminders: their revelry is merely a countdown before mortality strikes.

<!-- fact: Richard Jones's production favors clean lines and period fidelity, accentuating the bleak socioeconomic reality of 19th-century Parisian bohemians -->

## The Garret Revisited: One Last Fur Muff

In Act IV, a dying Mimì is brought back up the winding stairs to the room where they first fell in love.

<!-- fact: In Act IV, Mimì returns to the garret; Musetta pawns her earrings and Colline sells his overcoat to buy medicine and a muff for her cold hands -->

Musetta sells her earrings and Colline pawns his beloved old overcoat just to buy a fur muff so her fingers will stop shivering. Yoncheva rests her head against the pillow, her voice dropping to a fragile whisper: "My hands... are quite warm now."

<!-- fiction: evocative recounting of the opera's heartbreaking farewell -->

Emmanuel Villaume guides the Royal Opera House Orchestra into that crushing final chord. When the music ends, the entire theater seems to forget how to breathe.

<!-- fiction: quiet philosophical conclusion on the immortal grip of Puccini's tragedy -->
