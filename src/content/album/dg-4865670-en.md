## A Weathered Leather Suitcase on Gendarmenmarkt

If Berlin in the 1920s had a scent, it would be damp cobblestones, the pungent ash of stale cigars, and sour absinthe spilled across a cheap cabaret counter.

When Joana Mallwitz steps onto the podium at the Konzerthaus on Berlin's Gendarmenmarkt, she seems like someone who has abruptly wound the clockwork spring of history to its limit. When Deutsche Grammophon released *The Kurt Weill Album* in late summer 2024—subsequently capturing the **2025 Gramophone Orchestral Award**—the classical music establishment sat bolt upright: we had grown far too comfortable relegating Kurt Weill to the dim footlights of smoky theater pits, forgetting that beneath the greasepaint ran the blood of a formidable, uncompromising symphonist.

## Two Symphonies: Weimar Requiem and Exilic Sprint

The world recks Weill through *The Threepenny Opera* and "Mack the Knife," but Mallwitz turns the floodlights onto Weill's two standalone symphonies.

Symphony No. 1 (1921) was penned when Weill was barely twenty-one, fresh out of Ferruccio Busoni's masterclasses, as Berlin's streets heaved with post-war riots, hyperinflation, and existential disillusionment. Mallwitz unleashes the brass of the Konzerthausorchester with razor-sharp urgency. This is no lush late-Romantic sigh; it is the neurotic, jagged pulse of a modern metropolis grinding beneath factory smokestacks.

By Symphony No. 2 (1933–34), Nazi jackboots had shattered the shopfronts of Berlin, and Weill was an exile in Paris. Urged on by Bruno Walter, Weill produced this "Fantaisie symphonique." Under Mallwitz's surgical rhythmic grip, the central *Largo* unfolds like a monochrome funeral tango danced before the gallows, flute and cornet weeping over an unforgiving, dry timpani thud.

## The Seven Deadly Sins: Sisters Born of Falsehood

The centerpiece of the album is Weill and Brecht's final grand collaboration, the satirical *ballet chanté* *Die sieben Todsünden* (*The Seven Deadly Sins*).

Chanteuse Katharine Mehrling possesses an instrument born for Weill—smoky, dry-eyed, etched with cynical street wisdom. She portrays the split personality of Anna I (the pragmatic, money-driven sister) and Anna II (the sensual, emotional dancer) with chilling intimacy. The male quartet of the hypocritical "Family" droning moral platitudes from the back is met by Mallwitz's orchestra driving forward like a sleek, runaway black roadster tearing through the gilded sins of American boomtowns.

<!-- fact: Die sieben Todsünden (1933) features text by Bertolt Brecht detailing the journey of Anna I and Anna II through seven American cities to earn money for their family home, satirizing capitalist morality -->

This is no polite museum piece. It is an arresting, brass-clad testament to human vulnerability, longing, and historical fracture, brought ablaze under Joana Mallwitz's incandescent baton.
