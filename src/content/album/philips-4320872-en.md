## A Knock from the Stranger in Grey

In the bitter winter of 1791 in Vienna, freezing winds sculpted sharp icicles along the eaves of Rauhensteingasse.

Thirty-five-year-old Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart lay sick with swollen limbs in his modest apartment. In the dim candlelight, an anonymous, sombre messenger had delivered an advance fee, commissioning a Requiem Mass for an unnamed patron. Delirious with fever, Mozart grasped his quill, increasingly convinced that this unfinished score was the funeral mass Death had commissioned for his own burial.

<!-- fact: In the summer of 1791, Count Franz von Walsegg sent an anonymous messenger to commission a Requiem Mass to commemorate his late wife; Mozart died on December 5, 1791, leaving the incomplete score to be finished by his pupil Franz Xaver Süssmayr -->

Across recorded history, Mozart’s *Requiem* has worn many visages: Karajan’s grandiose cathedral gilding, Böhm’s monumental solemnity, or modern period ensembles’ bracing speed. Yet the version one returns to in the quietest hours of the night is Sir Neville Marriner’s 1991 recording on Philips with the Academy of St Martin in the Fields (ASMF).

<!-- fact: Released by Philips Classics in 1991 for the Mozart bicentenary (catalogue 432 087-2), this celebrated studio recording features the ASMF and Chorus under Marriner's direction using the traditional Süssmayr completion -->

## Tears and Forgiveness in the Lacrimosa

When the famous rising figure of the *Lacrimosa* begins its tearful, halting steps, every defense dissolves.

Those first eight bars were the last notes Mozart ever penned on this earth. After that eighth bar, the manuscript trails off into silence and ink.

<!-- fact: Mozart collapsed while writing bar 8 of the Lacrimosa and died shortly after midnight; the remainder of the movement was completed by Süssmayr -->

What makes Marriner’s reading so uniquely moving is his refusal to indulge in melodrama. Having shaped the sonic world of Miloš Forman’s film *Amadeus*, Marriner understood Mozart with intimate affection. He guides the Chorus of St Martin in the Fields not into shrieks of apocalyptic terror, but into a deep, dignified mourning. The strings touch the ear like autumn leaves falling softly across a headstone, while soprano Sylvia McNair floats notes of incandescent, cloudless purity.

<!-- fiction: Evoking the serene, chamber-like intimacy and radiant balance of Marriner's interpretation -->

In the *Kyrie* and *Dies Irae*, Marriner maintains exceptional chamber clarity. The dark timbre of the basset horns and bassoons cuts through the texture without swamping the intricate polyphony of the chorus.

<!-- fact: Mozart specifically eliminated flutes and oboes from the Requiem's orchestration, choosing two dark basset horns to establish its sombre, autumnal atmosphere -->

## A Lantern in the Dark

Why do we listen to Mozart’s *Requiem* in our most vulnerable moments?

Not to frighten ourselves with the terror of mortality, but to find peace at the threshold of life. In his darkest, most impoverished hour, Mozart bequeathed to humanity an offering of boundless beauty and grace.

<!-- fiction: Wally Records closing reflection on comfort and spiritual illumination -->

Marriner’s baton acts like a gentle oil lantern held in the chill of the valley. It does not blind, but it illuminates each step on the way home. When the final *Lux Aeterna* gently subsides into silence, what remains is not fear, but a quiet, comforted sigh of release.
