## The Wheel that Grinds the World

In all of classical music, nothing seizes the pulse quite like the opening detonation of "O Fortuna."

<!-- fiction: capturing the visceral physical impact of Orff's opening chord -->

A thunderous crack of timpani and the unified roar of two hundred voices shatter the darkness. Fortune appears not as a gentle muse, but as a colossal stone mill grinding kings, beggars, popes, and drunkards indiscriminately to dust.

<!-- fact: Carmina Burana is framed by the famous chorus "O Fortuna," based on 13th-century Latin secular poems discovered at the Benediktbeuern monastery -->

This landmark live concert from London's Barbican Centre, released on LSO Live under choral conductor Richard Hickox, remains a towering benchmark. Hickox never treats the score as cheap Hollywood trailer fodder; he understands that beneath the volcanic percussion lies the carnal, dirt-stained joy of medieval wanderers celebrating spring and drowning despair in ale.

<!-- fact: Released on LSO Live (catalog LSO0025), this reading remains one of the most streamed and downloaded choral albums in the world -->

## A Roasted Swan in the Tavern

In the second section, "In Taberna" (In the Tavern), the music plunges headlong from cathedral heights into the boisterous mud of worldly debauchery.

<!-- fact: Part II depicts monks and scholars gambling, drinking, and abandoning ascetic virtue in the local tavern -->

Few moments in vocal literature are as savagely comedic as the "Song of the Roasted Swan" (Olim lacus colueram). The tenor soloist is forced into an agonizing, shrill falsetto, narrating the plight of a bird sizzling on a spit: "Once I lived upon the lakes, fair and graceful... now black and roasted, I am turned on the spit for greedy teeth!"

<!-- fact: The tenor solo in Olim lacus colueram requires extreme tessitura and grotesque theatricality to convey the swan's lament -->

Hickox conducts with biting black humor. The shrill cackle of the winds and the sneering brass capture medieval humanity's defiant hedonism: if the plague may claim us tomorrow, let us drain every flagon tonight.

<!-- fiction: characterizing the black humor and existential defiance of the tavern scenes -->

## The Lily in the Mire and the Eternal Return

It is only in the third part, "Cour d'amours," that peace descends. In "In trutina," the soprano's pure, suspended line blooms like a white lily in the swamp, balancing virginity against the surrender to desire.

<!-- fact: The soprano solo "In trutina" (On the Scales) captures an innocent girl's hesitation before giving in to romantic passion -->

Then, the crushing wheel of Fortune turns full circle. "O Fortuna" strikes again, sealing the circular journey of human existence.

<!-- fiction: literary description of the circular architecture of fate -->

Listening to Hickox's performance is like taking a draught of raw medieval spirits. Life may be precarious and fate blind, but before the wheel rolls over us, we will shout, we will feast, and we will love with all our might.

<!-- fiction: concluding philosophical tribute to human vitality in the face of fate -->
