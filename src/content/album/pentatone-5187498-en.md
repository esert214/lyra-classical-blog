## A Severed Arm and Three Redemptions

The moment Paul Wittgenstein collapsed on the Polish front in the First World War, shrapnel severed his right arm.

<!-- fact: Paul Wittgenstein, brother of philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein, was wounded on the Polish front in 1914 and underwent amputation of his right arm -->

For a young concert pianist from one of Vienna's wealthiest families, losing a right hand felt like being stripped of one's soul. Yet in a Siberian prisoner-of-war camp, Wittgenstein drew keys onto wooden boards and practiced fanatically with his remaining hand. After the war, he used his family fortune to commission Europe's greatest composers: write concertos for my left hand.

<!-- fact: Wittgenstein practiced left-hand fingerings in a Siberian POW camp and later commissioned works from Ravel, Prokofiev, Bortkiewicz, and Richard Strauss -->

Ukrainian pianist Illia Ovcharenko calls this remarkable album *Manu Sinistra*—Latin for "left hand." The moment the music begins, it seems impossible that only five fingers are touching the keyboard. They cascade across the eighty-eight keys, constructing the acoustic resonance of a gothic cathedral.

<!-- fiction: first listening impression, five fingers forming an entire cathedral of sound -->

This is not an accommodation for impairment; it is an act of defiance.

<!-- fiction: thematic emotional thesis -->

## From Bortkiewicz's Elegies to Ravel's Mud and Fire

The album's most breathtaking revelation is the Second Piano Concerto by fellow Ukrainian Sergei Bortkiewicz. This forgotten jewel of late-Romanticism glows with Rachmaninoff-like melancholia and velvet orchestral warmth.

<!-- fact: The album features Sergei Bortkiewicz's Piano Concerto No. 2 Op. 28, premiered in Vienna in 1923, an exceptionally rare work on record -->

Under the baton of Ukrainian conductor Oksana Lyniv with the MDR-Sinfonieorchester, Ovcharenko plays with an inescapable yearning for homeland—a cry for dignity arising from soil ravaged by war.

<!-- fiction: poetic connection between Wittgenstein's historical trauma and contemporary Ukrainian artistry -->

Ravel's *Concerto in D major* plunges us into the trenches of the Western Front. Contrabassoon and lower winds murmur in the abyss before the solo piano erupts like artillery.

<!-- fact: Ravel's Concerto for the Left Hand opens in the darkest register of the contrabassoon before the piano enters with an explosive cadenza -->

Ovcharenko refuses to treat it as a circus stunt. His left thumb sings like a burnished mezzo-soprano above the storm, while the other four fingers weave tempestuous currents beneath.

<!-- fiction: description of left-hand technique where the thumb sings the melody while remaining fingers provide orchestral accompaniment -->

## Prokofiev's Irony and the Will to Live

Prokofiev's Fourth Concerto proved too modern for Wittgenstein, who famously wrote back: "Thank you for the concerto, but I do not understand a single note and I shall not play it."

<!-- fact: Prokofiev completed Piano Concerto No. 4 Op. 53 in 1931; Wittgenstein never performed it, and its premiere came only posthumously in 1956 -->

In Ovcharenko's hands, those dry syncopations and razor-sharp accents become a celebration of black humor. It feels like a wounded soldier stepping out onto the ruins to dance a defiant tango.

<!-- fiction: literary metaphor for Prokofiev's satirical energy -->

If life cuts your wings, you dance on one foot; if fate takes your right hand, you conjure the universe with the five fingers you have left.

<!-- fiction: philosophical concluding reflection -->
