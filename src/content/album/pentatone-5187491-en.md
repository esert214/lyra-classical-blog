## The Women He Killed

No opera composer understood how to "kill" his heroines better than Puccini.

<!-- fiction: opening commentary, slightly ironic -->

Mimì of La Bohème died. Tosca jumped. Cio-Cio-San took her own life. Liù of Turandot gave her life to keep a secret.

<!-- fact: Mimì dies of consumption (La Bohème); Tosca leaps to her death (Tosca); Cio-Cio-San commits suicide (Madama Butterfly); Liù takes her own life (Turandot) -->

Each of these women died center stage. And Sondra Radvanovsky—one of the great Puccini sopranos of our time—has brought them all back.

<!-- fact: Sondra Radvanovsky (b. 1969) is one of the most important contemporary Puccini sopranos -->

This Puccini: Heroines is a feast of arias: from Mimì, Tosca, and Cio-Cio-San to Turandot's Liù, all sung by a single voice.

<!-- fact: Pentatone PTC 5187 491, released 2026, recorded live in Chicago in February 2025 -->

## Mimì — Farewell

In La Bohème, Mimì sings "Donde lieta uscì" to the poet Rodolfo—"From this happy place I depart," a farewell.

<!-- fact: 'Donde lieta uscì' is Mimì's Act III farewell aria -->

When Radvanovsky sings this, there is no self-pity in her voice. She sings farewell, but with that gentleness of someone who knows she is dying and does not want to burden the one she loves.

<!-- fiction: psychological description of the interpretation -->

This isn't a woman crying. This is a woman teaching the man she loves how to go on living.

<!-- fiction: literary interpretation -->

## Tosca — Despair

"Vissi d'arte"—"I lived for art, I lived for love."

<!-- fact: 'Vissi d'arte' is Tosca's Act II aria -->

This is Tosca's prayer as she is being persecuted. Radvanovsky makes this prayer not devout but accusatory—"Lord, I did good all my life, why do you make me suffer like this?"

<!-- fiction: description of the interpretation -->

The weight carried in that sustained note is a trembling you can feel even through a speaker.

<!-- fiction: listening impression -->

## Cio-Cio-San — Waiting

"Un bel dì vedremo"—"One fine day we shall see."

<!-- fact: 'Un bel dì vedremo' is Cio-Cio-San's Act II aria, imagining her beloved's return -->

This is the cruelest moment in the opera: a woman waiting for a man who will never return. Radvanovsky sings it full of hope—because Cio-Cio-San truly believes.

<!-- fiction: interpretation of the work's irony -->

We, the audience who know the ending, watch her innocent expectation from below—and the tragedy doubles in weight.

<!-- fiction: audience perspective -->

## Liù — Sacrifice

"Tu che di gel sei cinta"—"You who are girt with ice." This is Liù's farewell in Turandot.

<!-- fact: 'Tu che di gel sei cinta' is Liù's Act III farewell -->

Liù would rather die than reveal Calaf's name. Radvanovsky's voice reaches the peak of the whole album here—not a virtuosic high note, but pure, fatal dignity.

<!-- fiction: description of the singing -->

After this aria, you understand why Puccini's women all die, yet none of them is a failure.

<!-- fiction: commentary -->

## One Voice, Four Women

After one album, the same throat carries you through four completely different lives.

<!-- fiction: summation -->

Radvanovsky's gift is that she does not imitate—she lets each character speak for herself. Mimì's softness, Tosca's ferocity, Cio-Cio-San's innocence, Liù's resolve—all from the same body, yet none repeats the others.

<!-- fiction: commentary on her vocal art -->

If you can buy only one Puccini aria recital, this is the closest thing to "complete."

<!-- opinion: subjective recommendation -->
