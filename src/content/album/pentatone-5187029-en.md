## Treating the Piano as Eighty-Eight Percussion Blocks

Béla Bartók once made a startling declaration: the piano is not primarily an instrument of song; at its core, it is a precision percussion instrument.

<!-- fact: In the 1920s, Bartók developed his philosophy of the piano as a percussive instrument, subverting the 19th-century Romantic tradition of cantabile bel canto touch -->

In his *Piano Concerto No. 1*, this philosophy erupts into an unstoppable sonic torrent. When fingers strike the keyboard, there are no Chopin-esque billows of velvet; instead, jagged syncopations rise directly from the damp soil of the Danube basin. It feels less like pressing ivory and more like an ancient artisan striking granite with a stone axe—sparks flying into the dark.

<!-- fiction: Evoking the raw, primal impact of the opening movement of Bartók's First Piano Concerto -->

Released by Pentatone in the autumn of 2023, this recording unites Pierre-Laurent Aimard, conductor Esa-Pekka Salonen, and the San Francisco Symphony to transform one of 20th-century pianism's steepest peaks into a crystalline mountain of pure light.

<!-- fact: Pentatone released the album in September 2023 (catalogue PTC 5187 029), featuring Bartók's Concertos Nos. 1 and 3 with Aimard and Salonen conducting the San Francisco Symphony -->

## From Savage Vitality to Exile's Twilight

What makes this album fascinating is the twenty-year chasm separating the two concertos.

The First was composed in 1926 in Budapest, by a Bartók in the prime of his powers, eager to dismantle polite European conventions with serrated rhythm and bite. The Third was written in the sweltering heat of a Manhattan sickbed in the summer of 1945—with the composer terminally ill, exiled, his life’s hourglass down to its final grains of sand.

<!-- fact: Bartók composed his Third Piano Concerto in the summer of 1945 in New York as a birthday gift for his wife Ditta Pásztory-Bartók; its final 17 bars were orchestrated posthumously by his pupil Tibor Serly -->

In the second movement of the Third, the *Adagio religioso*, that ferocious percussive force settles into an astonishing stillness reminiscent of morning birdsong. Aimard’s touch seems almost weightless beneath the acoustic canopy of Davies Symphony Hall, delivering a deeply affectionate farewell to the distant plains of Hungary.

<!-- fiction: Reflecting upon the profound, serene meditation of the Adagio religioso in exile -->

Salonen’s direction is equally inspired. He untangles the intricate contrapuntal web with surgical clarity, allowing woodwind dialogue and string mists to shimmer in lucid relief, governed by a cool Scandinavian restraint.

<!-- fact: Salonen is universally acclaimed for his interpretations of 20th-century and contemporary masterworks and serves as Music Director of the San Francisco Symphony -->

## When Silence Reclaims the Keys

Late at night with headphones, this recording cleanses away the clutter of everyday existence. Aimard uses no indulgent pedaling to blur edges; every note stands upright and luminous in the air.

As the final triumphant triplet of the Third Concerto dissolves, the solitary second of silence before the San Francisco applause is the most moving moment on the disc: an enduring peace found after a lifetime of turbulence and displacement.
