## Six Symphonies by a Man in Exile

How many homes does a man need to write six symphonies?

<!-- fiction: opening rhetorical question, literary device -->

Bohuslav Martinů's answer: all six symphonies were written after he left Bohemia.

<!-- fact: All six Martinů symphonies were composed between 1942–1953, during his American exile -->

He didn't flee his country. His country was taken from him—first by the Nazi occupation of Prague, then by the communist takeover that followed. He never returned to live there for good.

<!-- fact: Martinů left Europe for the United States in 1940; political circumstances prevented a permanent return to Czechoslovakia -->

This complete cycle, recorded by the Bamberg Symphony under Jakub Hrůša, is the first time DG has issued all six symphonies together.

<!-- fact: DG 486 7810, released 2026, DG's first complete Martinů symphony cycle -->

## No. 1 — The Shape of Homesickness

The First Symphony was written in 1942, shortly after Martinů settled in America.

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 1 was composed in 1942 during Martinů's early years in the United States -->

Its opening brass and percussion carry the rhythmic pulse of Czech folk music—that dual character, at once fiery and lonely, is Martinů's most distinctive signature.

<!-- fact: Martinů's music blends neoclassical structure with Czech folk elements -->

Hrůša doesn't play the Czech repertoire with excessive nostalgia. He keeps the rhythms clean and lets the hidden counterpoint surface. This isn't a man mourning his homeland—it's a man rebuilding it with music.

<!-- fiction: subjective description of the interpretation -->

## No. 4 — The One Called 'Liberation'

The Fourth Symphony, written on the eve of victory in World War II, is subtitled "Hymn to Liberation."

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 4 was written in 1945, conceived as a hymn to the liberation of Czechoslovakia -->

The trumpet theme that opens it is short, bright, like the first light of morning.

<!-- fiction: visual metaphor for the sound -->

This is the track I replay most often in the whole set. Hrůša lets the Bamberg brass sing with that trembling hope—not the clamor of triumph, but the still-unbelieving quiet of the moment the war actually ended.

<!-- fiction: personal listening impression -->

## No. 5 — The Shadow After the Light

If No. 4 is liberation, No. 5 is the look back after liberation.

<!-- fiction: interpretive framing -->

The Fifth Symphony was written in 1946, the first year of peace. There is no triumphant jubilation in the music—instead, a restless current. It's the bewilderment of knowing the war is over but not knowing what comes next.

<!-- fact: Symphony No. 5 was composed in 1946, a turning point toward Martinů's late style -->

In No. 5, Hrůša slows the tempo and lets the orchestral texture reveal more layers. The sustained low strings sound like a man sitting by a window at night, counting the lights going out one by one along the street.

<!-- fiction: literary description of the Fifth's atmosphere -->

## Why Don't We Know Martinů Better?

In the mainstream Western classical narrative, Martinů has long been filed under "secondary Czech composer"—behind Dvořák, Smetana, and Janáček.

<!-- fact: Martinů has long been marginalized in the Western repertoire, less known than his Czech contemporaries -->

But Hrůša, president of the International Martinů Circle, is changing that.

<!-- fact: Jakub Hrůša is president of the International Martinů Circle -->

He has written a book about Martinů. He conducts this complete cycle. He entrusts it to the Bamberg Symphony—an orchestra founded by the descendants of Czech musicians—to play Czech music.

<!-- fact: Hrůša authored 'Hrůša on Martinů' and has championed Martinů's music throughout his career -->

This is no coincidence. It's a long-overdue, gentle homecoming.

<!-- fiction: commentary on the programming rationale -->
