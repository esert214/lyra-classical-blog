## How Long Can a Song Resist?

In 1973, a song rang through the streets of Chile: *The People United Will Never Be Defeated!* A year later came the Pinochet coup, and the song became a password for exiles and resistance.

<!-- fact: The song ¡El pueblo unido jamás será vencido! is a Chilean protest anthem that became a resistance symbol after the Pinochet coup -->

Two years on, American composer Frederic Rzewski turned it into a set of piano variations: thirty-six of them, nearly an hour long — he turned one song into a marathon of resistance that demands real strength just to finish playing.

<!-- fact: Rzewski wrote The People United Will Never Be Defeated! in 1975, 36 variations on Sergio Ortega's theme -->

## An Album "Never Likely to Be Equalled"

In 1999, Canadian pianist Marc-André Hamelin recorded the work for Hyperion. A telling detail of dates: the session took place in August 1998, the release in April 1999 — the last year of the twentieth century.

<!-- fact: Recorded Aug 1998 at Henry Wood Hall, London; released Apr 1999 -->

Gramophone wrote something it rarely writes: "Never likely to be equalled." That phrase has sat on the album page for over twenty years with no reason to be removed.

<!-- fact: Gramophone Editor's Choice + Critics' Choice; the recorded quote is 'Never likely to be equalled' -->

## Thirty-Six Variations, Thirty-Six Transformations

The theme itself is only thirty-six bars, yet Rzewski makes it rotate through all twelve minor keys, climbing in building cycles of six. Listeners pass through waltz, march, jazz improvisation, and a fugue approaching Bach-like counterpoint — and at the very end, Rzewski leaves an empty slot for free improvisation.

<!-- fact: The 36-bar theme descends through a cycle of fifths across all 12 minor keys; the variations include a fugue, ending in an optional improvised cadenza -->

Hamelin chose to improvise, and in that instant quoted Eisler's *Solidaritätslied* — as if proving the Chilean song has always stood beside the workers of the world.

<!-- fact: Hamelin quotes Hanns Eisler's Solidaritätslied in his improvisation at the final cadence -->

## An Undying Pulse

Rzewski died in 2021, at 83. His whole life was spent on the impossible question of whether music can change the world; this album is one of the ways he handed the answer to you.

<!-- fact: Rzewski (1938–2021), American composer and pianist -->

Forty-plus years on, the piano on this album still burns. Some music expires; some music is waiting for a revolution that has not yet arrived — this Hamelin recording is clearly the latter.

<!-- fiction: poetic claim about the work's durability -->

## Further Listening

If Rzewski's political fire moves you, Ursula Oppens — who commissioned and premiered the work in 1975 — re-recorded it for the fortieth anniversary in 2015. And if you want to know everything a piano can do, Hamelin's recording of Alkan's Symphonie for solo piano is the entire answer in one sitting.
