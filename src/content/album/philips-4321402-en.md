## Blessed Are They That Mourn, for They Shall Be Comforted

In the canon of Western sacred music, almost every Requiem is composed for the dead.

From Mozart to Verdi, the Latin liturgy trembles before the terrifying fires of the *Dies Irae*, the judgment horn, and the sinner pleading on the brink of eternal condemnation. Yet at age thirty-five, Johannes Brahms shattered this medieval apparatus.

<!-- fact: Brahms discarded the traditional Catholic Latin text, including the Dies Irae, selecting his own passages from Martin Luther's German Bible to comfort those who remain behind rather than pray for departed souls -->

That period had claimed the two most essential anchors of his life: his mentor Robert Schumann, and his beloved mother Christiane. Sitting in a darkened apartment in Hamburg, Brahms opened his German Bible. His opening words did not plead for eternal rest; they invoked the Beatitude from the Gospel of Matthew:

"Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted."

<!-- fact: Composed between 1865 and 1868, the work was deeply spurred by the loss of his mother in February 1865 and Schumann's tragic death a decade earlier -->

## Shaking Off Victorian Velvet

For generations, large modern orchestras often buried *Ein deutsches Requiem* under a swamp of Victorian solemnity, dragging hundreds of choristers through mud-thick textures.

When Sir John Eliot Gardiner recorded this work in 1991 with the Orchestre Révolutionnaire et Romantique and the Monteverdi Choir, the classical music world stopped in its tracks.

<!-- fact: Released by Philips Classics in 1991 (catalogue 432 140-2), this was the first recording of Brahms's German Requiem using authentic 19th-century period instruments and historical pitch -->

Gardiner stripped away every ounce of sentimental glaze. Gut strings allowed the orchestral polyphony to breathe with chamber-like transparency; narrow-bore horns and wooden flutes cast shafts of morning sunlight through forest canopies rather than bludgeoning the ear. The Monteverdi Choir delivered the German consonants with incandescent, razor-edged diction, imbuing every syllable with raw spiritual urgency.

<!-- fiction: Contrasting the luminous clarity of period instruments with traditional modern orchestral heaviness -->

When Dutch soprano Charlotte Margiono sings in the fifth movement, "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you," her silvery, unvarnished tone brings tears to the eyes. It is not a distant deity speaking from high above; it is a child remembering the worn hands of his mother.

<!-- fact: The fifth movement ('Ihr habt nun Traurigkeit') was composed after the initial premiere as a direct personal memorial to his mother Christiane -->

## In a World Where All Flesh Is as Grass

Listening late at night, the second movement’s somber sarabande march ("For all flesh is as grass") reminds us of the terrible fragility of human existence.

Yet Brahms never abandons us to nihilism. In Gardiner's hands, the magnificent fugal climaxes reach upward toward blinding light, transforming our fear of death into an unyielding celebration of human endurance and love.

<!-- fiction: Wally Records closing reflection on finding comfort and peace in grief -->

We will all lose those we love, and we will all return to dust. But until that day comes, this recording places a warm, reassuring hand upon your shoulder in the dark, gently telling you: do not fear; morning will surely come.
