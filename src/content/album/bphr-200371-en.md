## A Blood-Stained Trilogy Under the Iron Curtain

Kirill Petrenko’s first major symphonic box set with the Berliner Philharmoniker targets the supreme crucible of 20th-century Russian music: Shostakovich’s Symphonies Nos. 8, 9, and 10.

Spanning the inferno of World War II (the 1943 Eighth "Tragic"), postwar clownish satire dodging state censorship (the 1945 Ninth), and the cataclysmic thaw following Stalin’s death (the 1953 Tenth), Petrenko dissects every note with surgical fury, unlocking terrified whispers, bitter irony, and monumental sorrow.

## Uncompromising Dynamic Extremes

In the colossal 25-minute opening movement of Symphony No. 8, when the battering-ram march reaches its apex with full brass and dual timpani delivering devastating blows, the Berlin Philharmonic’s weight is bone-shaking. Yet when the dust settles, the solitary English horn soliloquy wanders like a ghost across frozen battlefields.

The second movement of the Tenth Symphony—a four-minute cyclone of pure terror—is propelled at a ferocious tempo. Petrenko sculpts this terrifying portrait of totalitarian dread with relentless discipline and terrifying percussion thrusts.

<!-- fact: The furious second movement of Shostakovich’s Tenth is widely regarded as a musical portrait of Joseph Stalin -->

## The Survivor's Stubborn Motive

When Shostakovich’s personal monogram DSCH hammers triumphantly in the closing pages of the Tenth, it is not false state triumphalism we hear, but the resolute defiance of a human spirit rising from the ashes of tyranny.
